# What if Apophis hit Earth? — 5-minute faceless documentary script

**Format:** YouTube video, 16:9, 1080p (the narration works unchanged for a 9:16 reframe)
**Length:** 5:00 (about 700 spoken words, at roughly 145 words per minute, with room for visual-only beats)
**Structure:** hook → intro → body → recap → outro
**Pacing:** a new shot every 5–8 seconds in the first minute, then one evolving 30-second shot per beat

## Premise contract

> We change one thing: Apophis's path on its real flyby date, Friday 13 April 2029. We keep what is known about the asteroid: its size, its stony composition, and a realistic impact speed. We follow it from atmospheric entry to the blast zone. Then we answer one question: **how bad would an Apophis impact actually be?**

The impact is openly a hypothetical. The script says plainly that in reality Apophis misses, and that NASA has ruled out any impact for at least 100 years.

**Story route:** the clock starts (recipe 01, "Time zero"). The ocean beat borrows the "test the intuitive answer" body from recipe 26. Both use the same premise, units and observer, so they fit together.

## Narration

### HOOK (0:00–0:20)

What if, on Friday, April 13th, 2029, Apophis didn't miss?

This rock is real. It's about 340 metres wide, roughly the height of the Eiffel Tower. That date is real too: it's the day Apophis passes closer to Earth than many of our TV satellites.

In real life, it misses. Today, we take away the miss.

### INTRO (0:20–1:00)

Here's the rule for this experiment. We keep what we actually know about Apophis: a stony rock, 340 metres across, or a little over three football fields.

We change only one thing: its path. Instead of passing 32,000 kilometres above us, it meets the ground.

No one has ever weighed Apophis. Its mass is estimated from its size and the kind of rock it's made of, so every number that follows comes as a range.

And it arrives fast: about 12 and a half kilometres every second. At that speed you'd cross the Atlantic in under seven minutes.

### BODY

**Beat 1: entry (1:00–1:30)**

Ten seconds before impact, Apophis hits the top of the atmosphere.

Small space rocks never make it down. In 2013, an 18-metre asteroid exploded high above Chelyabinsk in Russia, and its shock wave blew out windows across the city.

Apophis is nearly twenty times wider. The air can't stop it. It punches through almost intact, pushing a wall of glowing, compressed air ahead of it.

**Beat 2: the energy (1:30–2:00)**

Impact.

In a fraction of a second, all that motion turns into heat and pressure. Our estimate is between one thousand and twelve hundred and fifty megatons of TNT.

That's more than twenty times the largest nuclear bomb ever tested, and roughly seventy thousand Hiroshimas, released at a single point.

The fireball alone grows to about seven kilometres across.

**Beat 3: crater and blast (2:00–2:30)**

When the light fades, there's a crater about five kilometres wide. The ground shakes as hard as a magnitude 6.6 earthquake.

But the real damage travels through the air. In our model, the blast wave flattens ordinary wooden houses out to about 50 kilometres, and shatters windows more than 100 kilometres away.

Over a major city, millions of lives would be at risk.

**Beat 4: what if it hit the ocean? (2:30–3:00)**

So would an ocean impact be worse? It feels obvious: a giant rock should make a giant tsunami.

But simulations show that most of the energy, about 80 percent, goes into boiling water and carving a temporary hole in the sea.

The waves are short and choppy, and in deep water they shrink quickly. They're most dangerous within about 100 kilometres of a coast.

**Beat 5: the rest of the planet (3:00–3:30)**

What about everyone else?

The asteroid that ended the dinosaurs was at least 10 kilometres wide, more than twenty-five thousand times the volume of Apophis.

Scientists put the line for worldwide effects, like dust dimming the sky and failing harvests, at around one kilometre.

Apophis is about a third of that. This would be a regional catastrophe, not the end of the world.

**Beat 6: would we even know? (3:30–4:00)**

And if it really were coming, would anyone tell us?

Apophis's orbit isn't a secret. Astronomers around the world, amateurs included, send their measurements to one shared database. Teams in the United States and in Italy calculate its path separately.

In 2025, when another asteroid's impact odds rose to about three percent, that was published while it happened. Days later, new observations brought it back down.

### RECAP (4:00–4:30)

So here's the chain. A 340-metre rock, moving 12 and a half kilometres a second, turns its motion into a blast of around a thousand megatons.

The crater is five kilometres wide. The destruction reaches tens of kilometres beyond it.

Whether that means thousands of victims or millions depends almost entirely on one thing: where it lands.

### OUTRO (4:30–5:00)

But this scenario has a flaw. We found Apophis in 2004, twenty-five years before this flyby. With that much warning, there's time to act, and in 2022 NASA's DART spacecraft proved we can push an asteroid off course.

The rocks that worry scientists are the ones nobody has found yet.

So on April 13th, 2029, if you're in Europe, Africa or western Asia, look up. You'll see Apophis cross the sky like a moving star.

## Sources and assumptions

**Facts**
- NASA, [Apophis Facts](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/asteroids/apophis-facts/):
  - 340 m mean diameter, long axis at least 450 m
  - passes 32,000 km from the surface on 13 Apr 2029, closer than many geosynchronous satellites
  - discovered 19 Jun 2004 at Kitt Peak
  - no impact risk for at least 100 years
  - visible to the naked eye from the Eastern Hemisphere
  - S-type (stony)
- The Planetary Society, [Will Apophis Hit Earth?](https://www.planetary.org/articles/will-apophis-hit-earth):
  - 2.7% odds of a 2029 impact after discovery
  - more than 1,000 Mt if it hit
  - widespread destruction up to several hundred km
  - visible from Europe, Africa and western Asia at magnitude 3.1
  - Chicxulub impactor at least 10–15 km
  - orbits computed at JPL (USA) and NEODyS (Italy) from Minor Planet Center data, including amateur observations
- Gisler ocean-impact simulations and the 2016 NASA–NOAA workshop: [Space.com](https://www.space.com/36451-asteroids-bad-at-making-waves.html), [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/57258-asteroid-impact-ocean-computer-simulations-solar-system.html), [Robertson et al., NASA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170005214/downloads/20170005214.pdf)
- 1 km global threshold and 140 m regional threshold: [National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25476/chapter/3), [OpenStax](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/13-2-asteroids-and-planetary-defense)
- 2024 YR4 odds peaking near 3% in Feb 2025, then ruled out: [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/02/250225122436.htm), [KTVU](https://www.ktvu.com/news/asteroid-2024-yr4-impact-chances-2032)
- DART shortened Dimorphos's orbit by 32 minutes: [JHU APL](https://www.jhuapl.edu/PressRelease/221011-dart-mission-impact-changed-asteroids-motion-in-space)

**Scenario assumptions and model estimates** (said aloud in the narration as estimates)
- Impact speed 12.6 km/s. This is an assumption: Apophis's approach speed of about 5.8 km/s combined with Earth's escape speed of 11.2 km/s.
- Density 2,600–3,200 kg/m³ for a stony body, giving 54–66 billion kg.
- Energy 1,015–1,249 Mt, final crater 4.6–4.9 km, fireball radius 3.2–3.5 km, seismic equivalent M 6.6–6.7, wooden houses collapsing (26.8 kPa) out to 50–54 km, windows breaking (6.9 kPa) out to 118–126 km.
  - These come from running the published Collins, Melosh & Marcus (2005) equations (the model behind *Impact: Earth!*) with a 45° impact into 2,500 kg/m³ rock.
  - They are model estimates, not measurements.
- Comparisons: Hiroshima taken as 15 kt; Tsar Bomba as 50 Mt.
- The 18 m Chelyabinsk object is about 1/19 of Apophis's width, so "nearly twenty times wider".

## Production handoff

- **Topic:** What if Apophis hit Earth? A hypothetical impact on the real 13 Apr 2029 flyby date.
- **Audience:** general science-curious viewers. They're scared but sceptical, distrust official reassurance, and are confused by the odds.
- **Duration:** 5:00. **Aspect ratio:** 16:9. **Resolution:** 1080p.
- **Visual direction:** cinematic, realistic space and terrain. Every illustrated event tagged "SIMULATION". A steady, deliberate camera; lock on the impact point at the moment of impact. Meters and feet on screen together.
- **Pacing:** 8 shots of 6–10 s in 0:00–1:00, then 8 single shots of 30 s each.
  - Each 30 s shot must keep changing (camera move, counters, expanding rings), never a static hold.
  - Most AI video models make clips of about 5–15 s. A true 30 s one-shot needs either a coded or 3D render (for example the Content Factory / Higgsedit route) or clips chained from each one's last frame, with the cuts hidden.
- **Opening:** "What if, on Friday, April 13th, 2029, Apophis didn't miss?"
- **Final payoff:** a regional catastrophe, not an extinction event. Where it lands decides the toll. In reality we saw it coming 25 years early, so look up on 13 April 2029.
